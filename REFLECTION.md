# What I Learned Directing an AI Agent (and When I Had to Overrule It)
*A short reflection on the human/AI division of labor · Alex Newton, 2026*

I set out to fix a boring problem — two weeks of travel photos with the wrong timezone stamped on every file and no location data — and ended up with a much more useful takeaway about how to actually work with an AI agent. The agent (Claude Code) wrote every line of code. I never touched a script. And yet the project would have failed without the human half of the work. Understanding *which* half is, I think, the real skill.

**Delegation is the easy part; specification is the hard part.** The agent is astonishingly capable at mechanics — ExifTool incantations, a raw-development pipeline, driving Hugin from the command line. What it can't do is decide what "good" means. Early on I made myself write down constraints that felt obvious but weren't: never modify my phone photos, keep everything reversible, verify against ground truth, make it re-runnable. Every one of those shaped the design. When I was vague, the agent filled the gap with a reasonable-but-wrong assumption. When I was precise, it executed flawlessly. That maps almost perfectly onto client work: the quality of the output is capped by the quality of the problem definition, and that's on me, not the tool.

**The agent optimizes for "technically correct." I had to hold the line on "actually right."** Two moments stand out. First, its color processing pushed each RGB channel through the tone curve independently — mathematically defensible, but it tinted the clouds faintly red in some places and green in others. I noticed it looked *off* before I understood why, and pushed until we diagnosed it as a per-channel hue shift and switched to tone-mapping luminance only. Second, its default panorama projection bowed the foreground shoreline into a distracting "smile." The agent was content with it; I wasn't. I compared alternatives, learned why wide fields of view distort, and had it choose the projection adaptively based on each scene. Neither fix required me to write code. Both required me to *care about the result more than the agent did* and to know enough to say "no, do it this way."

**"Trust but verify" has to be operational, not a slogan.** It's easy to let a confident agent's output wash over you. I forced the opposite. Before processing 38 panoramas, instead of eyeballing them, I had the agent measure exposure and clipping straight from the raw sensor data — which proved the scenes needed shadow recovery, not full HDR, and saved hours of the wrong approach. I checked the timestamp fix by comparing a corrected photo's UTC against the matching phone GPS reading (a 39-second delta — pass) and confirmed geotags landed within about eleven meters of the true spot. When I couldn't verify something automatically — the location of photos taken where neither the phone nor Google Timeline had signal — I didn't let the agent guess; I identified the places from the photos themselves and stamped conservative, neighborhood-level coordinates. The discipline wasn't "did it run?" It was "how do I know it's right, and what's my confidence?"

**Auditing the approach mattered as much as auditing the output.** Before committing, I had the agent check our whole method against how experienced photographers actually work — forums, the PanoTools documentation. That did two things: it confirmed the core pipeline was the canonical one (so I could trust it), and it surfaced a real-world failure mode we'd missed — low-texture skies and water can starve the stitcher of alignment points. So we changed the design to *flag* those cases for manual finishing rather than silently ship a broken panorama. Failing loudly beat failing invisibly.

**What surprised me** was how much the collaboration rewarded taste and judgment over technical depth. I am not a developer. But I could tell when an image looked wrong, reason about a tradeoff, insist on verification, and know when to go read what the experts do. The agent supplied the *how*; I supplied the *what* and the *whether*. The bottleneck was never the code.

If I had to compress it: **working effectively with AI is fluent delegation plus rigorous oversight.** Hand off the mechanics without ego; keep an iron grip on the problem definition, the quality bar, and the verification. Know what to check, how to check it, and when the confident answer in front of you is still wrong. That's not a photography skill or even a coding skill — it's exactly the judgment a good consultant or operator brings to any engagement, now amplified by a tool that can build almost anything you can clearly specify and rigorously check.
